# RosterBot v0.32 Phase 0A — Blind New-User Adoption Audit

## Audit boundary and method

This audit was conducted from the released `v0.31.0` baseline at commit `41e09a2c7c38654e51cbf4c5bdf05c8e72044a02`, on branch `experiment/v0.32-ux-adoption` in the separate `rosterbot-v0.32-ux-adoption` worktree.

I did not inspect application JavaScript, CSS, HTML source, tests, project documentation, roadmap material, commit history, persistence documentation, comments, or implementation notes. The observations below come from the shipped interface as a black box. The application was served locally, every non-local browser request was blocked, and no production account or Cloud data was accessed.

The primary journey used a fresh 390 × 844 phone viewport with empty local storage, IndexedDB, Cache Storage, service workers, and session state. Shorter fresh-state reviews used 768 × 1024 tablet and 1280 × 900 desktop viewports. I also tested setup abandonment and reopening, configured navigation, offline cold launch, and keyboard traversal. All data was synthetic.

The evaluation date presented by the application was 20 October 2026.

## 1. Executive verdict

RosterBot contains a genuinely strong product hidden behind an onboarding experience that asks users to understand too much before they have felt the benefit.

Once configured, it is immediately useful. The home screen answers “What am I doing today?”, shows the current fortnight, upcoming work/rest pattern, expected gross pay, and payslip-check status in one place. Opening My Roster reveals a full fortnight of duties, times, locations, rest days, and expected entitlement. That combination is the product’s necessity-maker: roster, diary, and pay cease to be separate manual chores.

The risk is reaching that point. A new user first sees a long setup form containing dates, employment classifications, schedule modes, depot, roster, line, alternate-roster logic, restore options, and unexplained abbreviations. The interface says it will show a roster, but it does not first demonstrate the broader payoff: today’s shift, next days off, expected pay, diary corrections, calendar usefulness, and offline access. “Roster Lookup” looks like a low-commitment route, but it leads to essentially the same detailed form.

My overall adoption assessment is:

- **Core value after setup: Strong — preserve.** The configured dashboard is focused, useful, and recommendation-worthy.
- **First-run value communication: High friction.** The product undersells its strongest benefits before asking for work.
- **Setup burden: High friction, approaching a critical blocker for reluctant users.** Knowledgeable drivers can complete it, but several decisions arrive before their necessity is apparent.
- **Trust once Data & Sync is found: Strong intent, moderate presentation friction.** The local-first and optional-Cloud messages are reassuring, but they are buried inside a long, technical panel.
- **Observed recovery interruption: Critical adoption blocker if it reproduces in a normal hosted first run.** A clean first-time confirmation twice displayed a “SAFE DATA RECOVERY” overlay immediately after the roster was accepted. Reopening then produced a normal configured dashboard. An immediate close after changing a setup field also reopened into recovery, while closing after a short pause resumed correctly. This audit does not diagnose the cause; it records the user-facing behavior.

A driver who reaches the configured home screen has a good reason to keep using RosterBot. A driver arriving cold may leave before seeing it.

## 2. First 30 seconds

### What I thought the app did

The large “LOCAL ROSTER TOOL / RosterBot” branding and “Let’s set up your role & schedule” heading made the broad purpose clear: it generates or tracks a train driver’s roster. It was not yet clear that it could also become a diary, calculate expected pay, check payslips, work offline, or help keep a phone calendar updated.

### What appeared most important

My eye went first to:

1. the large RosterBot brand;
2. “Let’s set up your role & schedule”;
3. the prominent `Roster Lookup` button;
4. the large “Already have a roster set up?” restore panel;
5. the beginning of the setup fields.

On a phone, `Show my roster` was well below the initial viewport. The restore/account block occupied valuable early space even though the primary persona was a new user.

### What I would tap first

I tapped `Roster Lookup`. It sounded temporary, safe, and lower commitment than setting up a personal roster. This was a reasonable new-user choice.

The result was disappointing as an adoption shortcut: the lookup still asked for starting date, role/pay classification, role effective date, schedule mode, depot, roster, line, and alternate-roster status. It did avoid saving, but it did not avoid the cognitive work.

### Immediate value and confidence

The value proposition was only partly obvious. “It shows my roster” was clear; “it brings my working life, pay expectations, diary corrections, and calendar together” was not.

I felt cautiously interested rather than confident. The explicit statement that nothing becomes the saved schedule until confirmation was reassuring. At the same time, the number of preselected fields created a new uncertainty: is it safe to continue with defaults, or will plausible-looking but incorrect choices produce misleading roster and pay information?

**Rating: High friction.** The first 30 seconds communicate function but not the full payoff.

## 3. Initial understanding of what RosterBot is

Before setup, my mental model was:

> “This is a roster generator. I tell it my classification, depot, roster, and line, and it shows my shifts.”

After setup, that model expanded substantially:

> “This is a personal work dashboard: today’s duty, fortnight roster, diary corrections, expected pay, payslip checking, calendar export/subscription, backups, and optional cross-device sync.”

That second description is far more compelling, but the first-run screen does not earn it. The app’s most powerful idea is not merely roster generation; it is combining roster, actual work, and pay in a single local-first reference that remains useful offline.

The configured experience also revealed two overlapping identities:

- `RosterBot` for roster, diary, calendar, and navigation;
- `PayBot` inside the Pay area.

The distinction may have history or personality value, but to a first-time user it briefly suggests a second product or mode that must be learned.

## 4. Time to first genuine value

There are two very different measurements.

### Artificial fastest path

If a user accepts every default without checking it:

- `Roster Lookup`;
- `Show roster`.

That is two taps and roughly 20–30 seconds to a real fortnight roster. It is fast, but it is not a responsible or realistic journey unless the defaults happen to be correct.

### Shortest realistic path

A realistic driver needs to understand or confirm:

1. starting date;
2. role/pay classification;
3. role effective date;
4. work-scheduling mode;
5. depot;
6. roster;
7. line;
8. whether they alternate with another roster/line.

This is one long setup surface followed by a preview/confirmation state, but on a phone it behaves like several screens of scrolling.

Estimated realistic journey:

- **Elapsed interaction time:** about 90 seconds to 3 minutes for a driver who knows all answers; longer if they are unsure about classification/effective dates.
- **Conceptual screens:** 2 before saved use — setup, then preview/confirmation.
- **Primary action taps:** 2 — `Show my roster`, then `Use this as my roster`.
- **Field interactions:** approximately 3–8, depending on how many defaults happen to match.
- **Required decisions:** 8.
- **Required fields:** 7 explicit values plus the alternate-roster choice.
- **Unfamiliar or compressed terms:** easily 10–15, including `WC`, classification codes, `PDT`, `DTCO`, `RDS`, `A/B/C/FLEX`, `FP68`, `OR`, `ALR`, and the distinction between role and roster dates.
- **Hesitation points:** at least 5 — the two dates, role code, schedule mode, roster/line correctness, and whether account/restore is relevant.

### First genuine payoff

The first genuine payoff was the preview after `Show my roster`: two clear weeks of actual duty codes, sign-on/sign-off times, duration, locations, and rest days. That is where the promise became concrete.

The stronger emotional payoff arrived after confirmation on the home dashboard. It combined:

- today’s work or rest status;
- the current fortnight pattern;
- total hours;
- expected gross pay;
- payslip-check status;
- a year-level diary/pay progress view.

Technical setup completion occurred at `Use this as my roster`. User reward began one step earlier, at the roster preview. The product should treat that preview as the conversion moment, not merely as validation.

## 5. Detailed first-run journey

### Step 1 — Landing

The landing surface was visually polished and calm. It did not look like a hobby form or an unsafe spreadsheet. The dark railway-themed presentation felt purposeful.

The first paragraph explained that RosterBot would show a roster before saving it. That was useful, although the full sentence was longer than most users will absorb on first scan.

**Strong — preserve:** preview-before-save and “nothing becomes your saved schedule until you confirm it.”

### Step 2 — Choosing the apparently easy route

`Roster Lookup` looked ideal for a reluctant adopter. It promised browsing without saving.

The route then presented almost the same setup burden. Being asked for a pay classification and its effective date during a “just looking” task felt disproportionate. My reaction was: “I thought I was only looking up a roster; why does it need my employment history?”

**High friction:** the low-commitment path is not low-effort.

### Step 3 — Reading the form

The form had good labels, generous controls, and helper text. The problem was not missing explanation; it was the amount of explanation and the sequence.

The two date questions were especially difficult:

- `Starting date` defaulted to today and displayed `WC 18 Oct 2026`.
- `Role effective from` also defaulted to today.

I did not know whether “starting date” meant employment start, roster cycle anchor, first date to display, or the date my current roster arrangement began. The second date then asked for a historical employment fact before I had seen any value.

The classifications mixed recognizable role names with payroll-style codes. A driver may know their role but not confidently know which code applies. The helper text explained why the role is separate from the roster, but that is a model the user has to learn before seeing PayBot.

The scheduling question was sound in principle, but it introduced `Official / master roster`, manual work pattern, no default pattern, trainees, `DTCOs`, `RDSs`, and `A/B/C/FLEX` in one area.

Depot, roster, and line were the easiest fields because they matched the expected job of the app.

### Step 4 — Backing out and resuming

`Change setup` returned from the preview without committing, and `Back` from Roster Lookup was understandable. A setup field changed to a different line remained selected after closing and reopening when I allowed a short pause first. This made ordinary abandonment/resume feel forgiving.

However, closing immediately after changing a field reopened into “Saved data could not be recovered automatically.” The screen said nothing had been reset and offered evidence export before clearing, which is commendably cautious, but a new user would not understand why any recovery was needed before they had even created a roster.

### Step 5 — Preview

The preview was excellent. It answered the central question: “Does this look like my working fortnight?” It showed enough detail to establish credibility without requiring another navigation decision.

The confirmation copy was good:

> “Does this look like your roster?”

`Change setup` and `Use this as my roster` made the next choices clear.

**Strong — preserve:** visible, reversible confirmation against real roster output.

### Step 6 — Confirmation

On two clean synthetic first runs, confirmation briefly produced a `SAFE DATA RECOVERY` overlay, even though the underlying dashboard had been built. The overlay described missing saved information and offered export/clear choices. Closing and reopening the browser then showed the normal configured dashboard without the overlay.

This is frozen as an observed black-box symptom, not an implementation diagnosis. A normal user would likely treat it as proof that setup had gone wrong or that the app could not be trusted with their data.

### Step 7 — Dashboard

The dashboard was the point at which RosterBot justified itself. It was much better than the onboarding had implied.

The strongest elements were:

- `My roster` with the current fortnight, line, week pattern, total hours, expected pay, and payslip status;
- `Today` with duty/rest status and times;
- `Pay checks` with a clear empty state;
- a year view with diary and payslip progress markers;
- familiar bottom navigation: Home, Roster, Calendar, Pay, More.

One wording issue reduced confidence: the card said `80.9 h recorded` even though no actual diary entries had been created. “Recorded” sounded like confirmed work rather than roster-derived or expected hours.

## 6. Abandonment points

| Point | Likely reaction | Risk | Has the user seen enough value? |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Restore/account panel before setup | “Do I need an account? Is this for existing users only?” | Moderate | No |
| Long phone form before the main CTA | “I can’t be bothered.” | High | No |
| `Roster Lookup` opening the same detailed form | “This was supposed to be the easy option.” | High | No |
| Starting date | “Starting what?” | High | No |
| Role/pay code | “I know my job, not necessarily this payroll code.” | High | No |
| Role effective date | “Why do you need that now?” | High | No |
| Schedule-mode explanation | “What applies to me?” | Moderate | No |
| Preselected defaults | “Will this quietly give me wrong pay?” | High | Not yet |
| Preview | “That’s actually useful.” | Low | Yes |
| Recovery overlay after confirmation | “It has already lost or broken my data.” | Critical | Just barely |
| First Pay screen | “This is powerful, but there is too much here.” | Moderate | Yes |
| Data & Sync technical language | “I don’t understand what is safe.” | High for Cloud adoption | Yes |
| Backup responsibility warning | “Will I lose everything?” | Moderate | Yes |
| Account/encryption concepts | “I’ll do this later.” | High for multi-device adoption | Yes |

The least-patient reasonable user leaves when `Roster Lookup` reveals that they still need to work through dates, role codes, and roster settings. A user who does not tap Lookup likely leaves when they see the length of the phone form and cannot yet see `Show my roster`.

## 7. Top adoption killers

### 1. A recovery warning during first setup

**Critical adoption blocker.** If reproduced in the hosted product, no copy improvement can compensate for a brand-new user being told their data needs recovery immediately after confirmation. The later successful reopen does not undo the trust damage.

### 2. The strongest value is shown after the largest demand for effort

**High friction.** RosterBot asks for eight decisions before showing the dashboard that makes those decisions feel worthwhile.

### 3. “Just looking” is not meaningfully simpler

**High friction.** The safe exploratory action still exposes employment and roster-model complexity.

### 4. Defaults look authoritative without being confirmed

**High friction.** Southern Cross, PB205, A roster, line 1, official/master roster, and today’s dates produce a polished, plausible result. A user can reach convincing but irrelevant output without realizing which assumptions mattered.

### 5. Two different kinds of “starting date” knowledge are required

**High friction.** The app needs a schedule anchor and a role-effective date, but the user has not yet been taught why those histories are separate.

### 6. Trust information arrives too late

**Moderate to high friction.** The app eventually explains local storage, no analytics, optional Cloud, and backup risk well. None of that is prominent when the user first sees account restoration and starts entering employment/roster information.

### 7. Technical safety language is visible to ordinary users

**High friction for Cloud adoption.** `Supabase`, session tokens, `schema-5 capability attestation`, payloads, revisions, encryption migration, and conflict language may all be responsible engineering terms, but together they shift the burden of understanding safety onto the user.

### 8. PayBot is valuable but visually and conceptually dense

**Moderate friction.** A huge expected-gross figure is compelling. The surrounding classifications, rate provenance, PH credits, rounding policy, input method, employment guarantee, fortnight controls, and 14 editable days compete with it.

### 9. Related personal setup is split across locations

**Moderate friction.** Roster changes live under Roster; employment and manual work patterns live under More; pay editing lives under Pay; actual work correction opens from Today/My Roster; backups and another-device use live under Data & Sync.

### 10. New-user safety can look like data fragility

**Moderate friction even without the observed recovery screen.** The backup panel correctly warns that clearing site data, private browsing, browser cleanup, or changing profiles can make data unavailable. Without an earlier concise explanation of the local-first model, the user may hear “this app can lose my roster.”

## 8. Strongest necessity-maker moments

### The configured home dashboard

This is the strongest feature and should be treated as the centre of the product. In one glance it can answer:

- what am I working today?
- am I off today?
- what fortnight am I in?
- roughly how much should I be paid?
- have I checked this payslip?

It feels like something worth opening every workday.

### The two-week roster with actual times and locations

Seeing duty code, sign-on, sign-off, duration, destination/location, and rest days laid out together produced the first “that’s handy” moment. It removes mental translation from a master roster.

### Expected pay attached to the same fortnight

The emotional payoff is not merely a number. It is the connection between roster and money. That invites the reaction: “I do not want to work this out manually anymore.”

### One-tap correction from Today

Tapping Today opens a detailed actual-day editor. The ability to record what really happened, notes, leave, times, and pay-related details suggests RosterBot can become a trustworthy diary rather than a static roster viewer.

### Calendar choices

The Calendar screen clearly separates an automatically updated subscription from a one-off export. “Put this in my phone calendar” is a practical recommendation trigger.

### Offline launch

The configured app cold-launched with network disabled and retained roster/pay information. This behavior is invisible during onboarding, but for train drivers in low-reception areas it is a real necessity-maker.

## 9. Features whose value is buried

- **Expected pay and payslip comparison** are absent from the initial promise even though they may be more persuasive than roster display alone.
- **Today’s duty** is one of the best recurring-use features, but the new user cannot see its shape before setup.
- **Offline availability** is highly relevant to the target environment but not used to build confidence during onboarding.
- **Actual-work diary and notes** appear only after interacting with a day.
- **Calendar automation/export** is a top-level tab after setup but is not part of the initial value proposition.
- **No-account local use** is explained thoroughly only in Data & Sync.
- **Optional encrypted cross-device sync** is available but wrapped in enough technical detail that a normal user may postpone it indefinitely.
- **Roster Lookup without changing saved state** is useful, but its setup requirements undermine its exploratory value.
- **Roster history and dated changes** are powerful for long-term accuracy but sound advanced before the basic recurring benefit is established.
- **Source transparency for pay rates and agreements** is available under More, which can strengthen trust, but a new user will not know to look there.

## 10. Navigation findings

The bottom navigation is familiar and mostly successful. Returning after two weeks, I could find the common jobs as follows:

| User question | First place looked | Result | Interactions / wrong turns |
|---|---|---|---|
| What am I working today? | Home | Immediately visible | 0 |
| What am I working tomorrow? | My roster card | Found in fortnight view | 1 |
| What am I doing next week? | My roster card | Found; second week already visible | 1 |
| When are my next days off? | My roster card | Found by scanning `OR` days | 1; `OR` must be known |
| What did I work last fortnight? | My roster, then previous arrow | Found | 2; arrow has no explanatory text |
| What should I be paid? | Home, then Pay | Amount visible immediately; detail in Pay | 0–1 |
| What has RosterBot put in my phone calendar? | Calendar | Choice/setup found | 1; current publication state requires reading further |
| How do I correct something that changed? | Today/My roster day | Day editor opens | 1–2 |
| How do I change my roster/setup? | Roster | `Add roster change` is clear | 1–2 |
| How do I change employment/pay details? | Pay first, then More | Found under `Employment & Pay Profile` | 2 plus one wrong turn is plausible |
| How do I back up my data? | More → Data & Sync | Found | 2 |
| How do I use RosterBot on another device? | More → Data & Sync | Possible path found, but not named in user terms | 2 plus substantial reading |

The top-level structure works best for daily questions and worst for profile/data-management questions. `More` is a reasonable home for secondary tools, but it contains materially important items: employment accuracy, backup, and cross-device use.

The search bar on Home is visually dominant after setup. Its placeholder says it can search duty, sign-on time, or date, but it has no introductory label and shows a clear `×` even when visually empty. It may draw attention away from the more valuable Today and My Roster cards.

## 11. Information-architecture findings

### What is clear

- **Roster** means the saved schedule and roster changes.
- **Calendar** means sending roster information to a calendar, either continuously or once.
- **Pay** means expected gross, details, and payslip checking.
- **Settings** means theme and default roster view.
- **Data & Sync** means backups, account, and optional Cloud.

### What is not clear

- **Diary** is a core product concept but not a top-level destination. It appears as the day editor, progress state, and personal roster history. A new user may not know whether “Roster” and “Diary” are separate or whether editing a roster day creates diary history.
- **Employment & Pay Profile** lives under More, not Pay, although it directly controls pay interpretation.
- **Manual work pattern** appears during initial roster setup and again in Employment & Pay Profile, making the ownership of that setting hard to understand.
- **RosterBot versus PayBot** creates a second conceptual boundary inside one application.
- **Data & Sync** groups three different user jobs: keeping a backup, moving to another device, and setting up Cloud. The grouping is technically sensible but cognitively broad.
- **Roster Lookup**, **View my roster**, **Browse official rosters & shifts**, and **Roster History & Changes** are distinct, but their differences take reading.

The interface is partly organized around user jobs and partly around the product’s internal concerns. Home is strongly job-oriented. Data & Sync and the full Pay screen feel more like the software exposing its model.

## 12. Wording and terminology findings

Train drivers should not be patronized; industry terms can be appropriate. The issue is not that RosterBot uses domain language. The issue is that too many terms appear before context establishes which ones matter.

### Clear wording

- `Show my roster`
- `Does this look like your roster?`
- `Change setup`
- `Use this as my roster`
- `Today`
- `Expected gross`
- `Check payslip`
- `Export calendar once`
- `Cloud Sync is optional`
- `Creating/signing in never uploads your roster by itself`

### Wording that caused hesitation

- `Starting date` — starting which relationship or calculation?
- `WC 18 Oct 2026` — compact and probably familiar to some drivers, but unexplained at first use.
- `Role effective from` — necessary-sounding historical administration before visible benefit.
- `Official / master roster` — are these synonymous, and how does the user know theirs?
- `I alternate with another roster/line` — understandable to insiders, but high consequence if misunderstood.
- `Local-first HTML/JavaScript` — developer information, not a user benefit.
- `80.9 h recorded` — sounded like confirmed actual work although only a roster had been configured.
- `Additional pay equivalent`, `PH credits movement`, `entitlement APR`, and detailed payroll-rounding language — accurate-sounding but cognitively heavy.
- `Cloud Sync SAFE SYNC` — reassuring intent, but the label feels like a claim that requires explanation.
- `schema-5 capability attestation` — not appropriate as primary ordinary-user copy.
- `.rosterbot file` — meaningful only after the backup format has been introduced.

## 13. Trust and privacy perception

### Trust builders

- The preview explicitly says no saved schedule is created until confirmation.
- Data & Sync says the app works without an account.
- It states that the browser remains the working copy.
- It says Cloud Sync is optional.
- It says creating/signing in does not itself upload the roster.
- It distinguishes the password from roster payloads and explains that the password is not written into RosterBot storage.
- It says there are no RosterBot analytics or tracking code.
- Feedback explains which diagnostics are included and says calendar contents are not sent.
- The calendar subscription warns that the private URL should be treated like a password.
- Recovery wording says nothing has been reset and offers evidence export before clearing.

### Trust gaps

- The landing screen asks for employment classification and roster details without first saying, concisely, where they will be stored.
- `Sign in & restore` is visually prominent before the user knows an account is optional.
- The user has to find Data & Sync to learn the strongest privacy facts.
- The local-storage warning is honest but long enough to make the system feel fragile.
- Supabase, tokens, encryption migration, revisions, conflict handling, and capability attestation make the user responsible for understanding implementation safety.
- The recovery overlay during clean onboarding overwhelms every other trust signal.
- The large pay estimate can look authoritative before the user has learned what is estimated, what is roster-derived, and what requires actual diary input.

A normal user can eventually answer “Where is my data?” but only after choosing to open a panel that initially looks like an advanced feature.

## 14. Error and empty-state findings

### No roster configured

The empty state is not blank. It provides a setup route, a lookup route, and restoration choices. This is fundamentally good.

The weakness is prioritization: the new-user task, exploratory task, and returning-user recovery task compete on one surface.

### Partial setup

With a normal short pause, a changed line survived close/reopen and setup resumed. This is forgiving.

With an immediate close after the field change, the next launch showed Safe Data Recovery. This is a realistic mobile behavior: users switch apps or close a tab without waiting for a save indicator.

### No diary data

The home screen still gives useful roster/pay information. The visual legend distinguishes diary/payslip progress. However, `recorded` can imply actual diary completion when the state is still roster-derived.

### No pay checks

This empty state is strong:

> “No payslips checked yet”

> “Compare a payslip with expected pay”

> “Open Pay when your payslip arrives”

It explains both absence and next action.

### Day editor with no actual record

The editor says no retrospective actual record is saved, which is helpful. For a rostered-off day, however, `Actual status` initially presented `Worked` at the top of its choices. Seeing “Worked” beside a day visibly marked `OR` created hesitation about whether opening or saving could accidentally assert work.

### Offline

The configured app launched and remained useful offline. There was no corruption language. In a purely local/no-account state, there was also no obvious global offline indicator. That may be appropriately quiet, but a user entering Calendar, feedback, or Cloud functions needs clear network-specific guidance at the moment of action.

### No Cloud account

The app clearly says Cloud is optional and local use continues. The panel is long, and the ordinary next step is surrounded by technical explanation.

### Recovery

The recovery UI is cautious and specific, but it is inherently frightening. Terms such as missing storage keys, schema markers, and evidence belong behind technical details, not in the emotional centre of a first-run failure.

## 15. Mobile, tablet, and desktop observations

### Phone

The configured experience is at its best on the phone:

- large, clear cards;
- useful bottom navigation;
- generous touch targets;
- a strong Today card;
- a readable My Roster card;
- no dependence on network for normal launch.

The first-run experience is weaker:

- one long vertical form;
- the main CTA below the fold;
- restore/account choices consume early vertical space;
- helper text creates substantial scrolling;
- the user cannot see the promised roster while deciding whether the setup is worth completing.

The Pay screen is extremely long and dense on phone. It is capable, but the important number competes with explanation and editor controls.

### Tablet

The two-column setup layout was the most balanced. It retained readable labels and helper text while reducing scrolling. The CTA was still below the initial viewport but not far away. The restore panel remained visually prominent.

### Desktop

The setup fit into a compact four-column layout, and the primary CTA was visible without much scrolling. This reduced physical effort but increased simultaneous cognitive load. Helper copy became small and dense, and the eye had to move across several related decisions in one row.

The product clearly has a mobile-first visual language, which suits the audience. Desktop is usable but feels like a compressed version of the same model rather than a separately optimized decision flow.

## 16. Accessibility and cognitive-load observations

This was an observation pass, not a formal standards audit.

### Positive observations

- Major controls and cards generally had large tap areas.
- Button labels were usually textual rather than icon-only.
- The Data & Sync close button had an accessible close label.
- First-run keyboard order was broadly logical: brand/home, lookup, restore, import, then form fields.
- Calendar/diary progress did not rely on color alone; filled/hollow states and a legend were present.
- Error/recovery wording provided explicit actions rather than a blank failure.

### Problems observed

- On the configured home screen, visually prominent section titles were not exposed as heading roles in the browser accessibility query. This makes structural navigation harder for screen-reader users.
- Opening Data & Sync did not move keyboard focus into the modal. Focus remained on the background Data & Sync card; subsequent Tab presses moved through background Settings, What’s new, and Feedback before reaching the modal close button. The modal did not behave as a contained keyboard task.
- Some card buttons expose very long accessible names containing the full card contents. That is descriptive but may be tiring to navigate.
- Secondary explanatory text is comparatively small and low-emphasis against the dark theme, particularly in dense desktop and Pay layouts.
- The setup and Pay screens impose high cognitive load through abbreviations, date logic, multiple modes, and long explanatory blocks.
- Several choices depend on understanding chronology and payroll concepts rather than simply answering a familiar question.
- The `×` symbol appears in several places; modal close controls need a consistent spoken label and clear purpose.
- The application uses strong color coding, but some states still require learning a legend and industry abbreviations.
- Large-text behavior was not formally exercised; the dense Pay surface and multi-column desktop setup are the areas most likely to become difficult.

## 17. Persona comparison

### Persona A — reluctant adopter

> “Someone at work told me this is useful. Convince me.”

This person taps Roster Lookup because it appears noncommittal. They are then asked for role and schedule history anyway. They are likely to leave at `Role effective from` or when they realize `Show roster` is below a long form.

If they reach the preview, they are much more likely to continue. The dashboard could convert them, but the product makes them pay the setup cost before showing it.

**Outcome:** not reliably converted.

### Persona B — practical regular driver

> “Just tell me what I’m working and what I’m getting paid.”

This person recognizes depot, roster, and line, tolerates the setup, and receives an excellent result. Home answers today and expected pay. My Roster answers tomorrow, next week, and next days off. Pay gives detailed confidence when needed.

They may still be confused by role-effective dates, `recorded` versus expected hours, and the split between Pay and Employment & Pay Profile.

**Outcome:** likely retained once configured.

### Persona C — power user

> “Give me Cloud Sync, calendar integration, backups, detailed pay and every useful option.”

RosterBot offers substantial depth: roster history, dated employment changes, actual-day records, notes, leave, allowances, payslip checks, calendar subscription/export, backups, encryption, and Cloud Sync.

The depth is not hidden from this user, but it is distributed. They need to learn Roster, Pay, Calendar, More, Employment & Pay Profile, and Data & Sync. The technical detail may reassure them, though some product actions are buried inside explanatory text.

**Outcome:** strongly served after discovery.

### Progressive-disclosure verdict

Persona A is forced to confront some Persona C complexity too early, especially employment chronology, schedule modes, restore choices, and Cloud-adjacent concepts. Persona C has plenty of depth but must learn where the product has distributed it. Persona B receives the best experience because the dashboard matches their daily questions directly.

## 18. Top ten adoption improvements — product/UX level only

These describe desired user outcomes, not implementation solutions.

1. **Lead with the complete benefit.** Tell a new driver that RosterBot can show today’s duty, upcoming days off, expected pay, and calendar-ready shifts—not merely “set up your role and schedule.”

2. **Show value before full commitment.** Let the user experience a believable sample or a truly minimal roster result before asking for employment history and advanced schedule decisions.

3. **Make the first setup progressive.** Ask only what is necessary to produce the first useful roster, then introduce pay classification, historical dates, alternation, Cloud, and advanced patterns when their benefit is visible.

4. **Turn setup into a confidence journey.** Clearly show progress, why each answer is needed, whether the default is only a suggestion, what happens if it is wrong, and that it can be changed later.

5. **Make “Roster Lookup” genuinely low effort.** Its product promise should be temporary exploration, not almost the same personal setup under another label.

6. **Put a concise trust promise near the first personal question.** Communicate that no account is required, the working copy stays on the device, and nothing is uploaded merely by entering roster details.

7. **Organize secondary setup around user jobs.** A user should be able to say “my roster changed,” “my role changed,” “my payslip arrived,” “I got a new phone,” or “back this up” and find one obvious path.

8. **Keep technical safety details available but progressive.** Ordinary copy should explain outcomes in plain language; protocol, schema, token, revision, and provider details should remain available for users who want evidence.

9. **Clarify expected versus actual information.** Labels for hours, pay, diary completion, and day status should make it immediately clear what came from the roster, what the user confirmed, and what remains an estimate.

10. **Treat accessible task containment as part of trust.** Modal focus, meaningful heading structure, large-text resilience, concise accessible names, and predictable close/back behavior should make complex areas feel controlled rather than overwhelming.

The observed first-run recovery interruption should be investigated before ordinary adoption improvements are judged, because it can invalidate every other onboarding gain.

## 19. Things that are already excellent and must not be accidentally redesigned away

- **The configured home dashboard.** It is the clearest expression of RosterBot’s value.
- **Preview before save.** Users can verify a real roster before committing it.
- **The two-week roster visualization.** Duty, time, duration, location, and rest days are easy to scan.
- **Today as a first-class card.** It answers the most common recurring question.
- **Expected pay beside roster context.** The connection is emotionally powerful and practically useful.
- **Clear no-payslip-yet empty state.** It explains what to do and when.
- **Bottom navigation.** Home, Roster, Calendar, Pay, More is familiar on mobile.
- **One-tap day editing.** It makes the app capable of tracking reality, not just predictions.
- **Calendar choice.** Automatic subscription versus one-off export is presented as a meaningful user choice.
- **Local-first, no-account operation.** The app remained useful offline and did not force Cloud adoption.
- **Explicit Cloud consent.** Signing in is not described as an automatic upload.
- **Backup and recovery caution.** The product tries to preserve evidence rather than casually clearing state.
- **Responsive presentation and touch sizing.** The phone dashboard feels like an app, not a desktop page squeezed onto mobile.
- **Depth for power users.** Roster history, pay audit, leave, actual duties, notes, calendar, backup, and Cloud can support long-term use.
- **Honesty about estimates and external behavior.** Pay is labelled as an estimate, and calendar refresh limitations are explained.

## 20. Final answers

### Why would a normal driver keep using RosterBot after trying it once?

Because, once configured, it gives them one dependable place to answer the questions they repeatedly care about: what am I doing today, what am I doing next, when am I off, and what should I be paid? It turns an abstract master roster into personal dates and times, connects that roster to expected pay, lets reality be corrected in a diary, can feed a phone calendar, works without reception, and keeps the current fortnight visible at a glance.

The recommendation-worthy sentence is:

> “It shows my actual fortnight and expected pay together, and I can still open it when I have no reception.”

### Why might they never make it that far?

Because RosterBot asks them to understand its data model before it proves its value. A cold user sees dates, classifications, codes, schedule modes, restore options, and a long form while the compelling Today/pay/dashboard experience remains hidden. The easiest-looking exploration path is not materially easier. Trust information is buried. If the observed Safe Data Recovery interruption appears during a real first run, it can end adoption immediately.

The abandonment sentence is:

> “I only wanted to see whether this was useful; I’m already being asked questions I’m not sure how to answer.”

## Brutal product-question answers

- **Would I finish setup with no emotional investment?** Only if a trusted colleague had already shown me the result or I immediately recognized every roster field. From the link alone, probably not.
- **Exact least-patient exit point:** after tapping Roster Lookup and discovering the role-effective-date and full roster setup, or before reaching the below-fold Show my roster button.
- **Does Home answer the most important question?** Yes. Today and the current fortnight are excellent.
- **Does RosterBot demonstrate value before requesting effort?** Not adequately. It promises roster output but does not demonstrate the broader daily/pay payoff.
- **Is the strongest feature obvious within 30 seconds?** No. The configured dashboard is the strongest feature, and it is not previewed.
- **What does RosterBot make the user understand that it should understand instead?** Which dates anchor which histories, how payroll classifications map to ordinary role names, which scheduling model applies, and several distinctions between rostered, recorded, actual, local, synced, encrypted, and attested state.
- **Which screen feels most developer-designed?** Data & Sync, followed by the full Pay screen. Both expose internal concepts and completeness before the user’s immediate decision.
- **Which feature should become the centre of the product?** The Home combination of Today + current fortnight + expected pay.
- **What would make a driver recommend it?** Accurate shift times, upcoming days off, expected pay, and offline availability in one phone-friendly view, with calendar integration as a practical bonus.
- **What could kill adoption despite excellent software?** Setup density before payoff, unclear confidence in defaults, technical trust language, and any first-run recovery warning.
